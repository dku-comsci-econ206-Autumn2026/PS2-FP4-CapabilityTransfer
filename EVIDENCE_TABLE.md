## How to read this table

**What "open" means here.** We follow Epoch AI's operationalisation: **"open" models are those with downloadable model weights (including those with restrictive licences), while "closed" models are either unreleased or can only be queried in an API or hosted service.** Epoch states this explicitly and notes that "open-weight models" is used as shorthand "whether or not the model's code or data is open," and that there is "some controversy over the definition of open source AI, and whether open weights are sufficient" (source: Epoch AI report, below). This is why the table records the **licence**, not merely weight availability, and why we mark cases where weights are downloadable only for research or only below a revenue/MAU threshold.

**Certainty keys.** Each factual cell is tagged:

- `observed` — stated on an official first-party page (company release note, model card, licence file, official documentation, or the developer's own technical report) that we opened.
- `reported` — credible third-party reporting, with the outlet named.
- `contested` — alleged or disputed; we name the accuser and, where one exists, the denier. Nothing tagged `contested` is presented as established fact.
- `none found` / `observed as absent` — a documented negative: we searched the sources listed and did not find the item. This is not proof of absence.

**Mapped decision legend.** ① closed + distilled (free rider). ② open + distilled (passer-on). ③ closed + trained (monopolist). ④ open + trained (source). Because the model's two axes are build-method and release choice, a single family frequently occupies more than one cell; where the build-method axis is not publicly documented we say so instead of guessing.

## Verified external anchors

**1. Epoch AI, "Open vs. closed AI: How behind are open models?" (report, now titled "How far behind are open models?"), 4 November 2024** — https://epoch.ai/publications/open-models-report (opened 2026-10-03). Authors: Ben Cottier, Josh You, Natalia Martemianova, David Owen. Verified findings, quoted:

- Definition of open vs. closed as given above (verified in the "What is an 'open' AI model?" section).
- "In terms of benchmark performance, the best open large language models (LLMs) have lagged the best closed LLMs by 5 to 22 months."
- "In terms of training compute, the largest open models have lagged behind the largest closed models by about 15 months."
- "Our overall finding is that in recent years, once-frontier AI capabilities are reached by open models with a lag of about one year."

Two **later Epoch data insights** show the lag has narrowed since, and they should be cited instead of the 2024 report whenever a current figure is wanted:

- 30 October 2025: "Frontier open-weight models lag behind the most capable models by an average of 3 months in the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI)... an average ECI gap of around 7 points, similar to the gap between o3 and GPT-5." (https://epoch.ai/data-insights/open-weights-vs-closed-weights-models, opened)
- 29 May 2026: "Since January 2026, the most capable open-weight models have lagged frontier closed models by an average of four months in the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI)... The average ECI gap was 8 points, similar to the gap between GPT-5 and GPT-5.5." (https://epoch.ai/data-insights/open-closed-eci-gap, opened)

Epoch's underlying source is its Notable AI Models database, cited on the report as https://epoch.ai/data/ai-models. That explorer is JavaScript-rendered and we could not read the table download directly, so **no per-model Epoch figure is quoted anywhere in this file** — only the aggregate statements above, which appear in the report and insight pages we opened.

**2. Mahyar Habibi, "Open Sourcing GPTs: Economics of Open Sourcing Advanced AI Models," arXiv:2501.11581 (submitted 20 January 2025, econ.GN)** — https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11581 (opened 2026-10-03). The paper exists and is verified. Its abstract states, verbatim:

> "This paper explores the economic underpinnings of open sourcing advanced large language models (LLMs) by for-profit companies. Empirical analysis reveals that: (1) LLMs are compatible with R&D portfolios of numerous technologically differentiated firms; (2) open-sourcing likelihood decreases with an LLM's performance edge over rivals, but increases for models from large tech companies; and (3) open-sourcing an advanced LLM led to an increase in research-related activities. Motivated by these findings, a theoretical framework is developed to examine factors influencing a profit-maximizing firm's open-sourcing decision. The analysis frames this decision as a trade-off between accelerating technology growth and securing immediate financial returns. A key prediction from the theoretical analysis is an inverted-U-shaped relationship between the owner's size, measured by its share of LLM-compatible applications, and its propensity to open source the LLM. This finding suggests that moderate market concentration may be beneficial to the open source ecosystems of multi-purpose software technologies."

Note for citation accuracy: the paper says the **inverted-U prediction is a theoretical prediction**, while the **"decreases with performance edge" result is an empirical finding**. Our P1 and P2 below preserve that distinction.

**3. Government-level distillation dispute (added because it bears directly on the paper's policy argument).** On 8 September 2026 the NSA, CISA and FBI issued joint Cybersecurity Advisory **AA26-251A**, "China-Based Artificial Intelligence Companies Conducting Industrial-Scale Distillation Campaigns Against U.S. AI Companies" — https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa26-251a (opened 2026-10-03). It alleges that "DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun, and Z.AI extracted billions of tokens across millions of exchanges/requests from U.S. frontier AI models, including variants of Claude, GPT, Gemini, and Grok, since at least late 2024," that "DeepSeek has conducted organized campaigns since at least 2024 targeting reasoning capabilities... to train its R1 and V3 models," that "Alibaba leveraged industrial-scale distillation to improve the company's Qwen family," and that such campaigns "form the core—not merely a supplement—of their AI development strategy." It further states that actors access models by "violating U.S. AI companies' terms of use." On 9 September 2026 China's Ministry of Commerce rejected the advisory: the allegation is "groundless and legally unsound," distillation "is intrinsically a neutral technical means," and "Reports from U.S. companies on their own model development also disclose their extensive distillation of Chinese models." No adjudication was found. Everything in the distillation column below is tagged accordingly.**

## A. What the table tests

Two testable claims drawn from the published literature on why firms open source, each stated as a prediction, followed by what this table can and cannot say about it.

**P1 — the propensity to release weights falls as a model's performance edge over rivals grows.** This is the empirical finding (2) of Habibi (2025), arXiv:2501.11581: "open-sourcing likelihood decreases with an LLM's performance edge over rivals, but increases for models from large tech companies." Verified verbatim from the abstract (opened 2026-10-03). Note that Habibi's finding is an empirical result and is paired with a second, opposite-signed effect for large technology companies, so a test of P1 alone is a test of one coefficient, not of the paper.

**Is the table consistent with P1?** Weakly yes, and only in the direction of the sign, in four of eight families. OpenAI keeps GPT-6 Astra closed while the open model it does release, gpt-oss-120b, is positioned in OpenAI's own launch post as reaching "near-parity with OpenAI o4-mini" — a previous-generation, smaller sibling — rather than parity with its current flagship. Meta does the same thing more starkly: the closed Muse Spark is the frontier model while the released Muse Glimmer is a 30-billion-parameter model distilled down from it. Anthropic, which competes at the frontier, has never released weights at all. Mistral labels its hosted models "Premier" while releasing Apache-2.0 weights. Against this, **DeepSeek is an apparent counterexample**: it releases MIT weights for models it presents as frontier-competitive (the V4 series), and the NSA/CISA/FBI advisory itself asserts that industrial-scale distillation "significantly shorten[s] AI development timelines and reduce[s] financial expenditures," which is a hypothesis about why a firm with a narrower edge might open. Google is a partial counterexample in the other direction: it moved Gemma to Apache 2.0 in April 2026 (its most permissive open release) while keeping Gemini closed, without a visible change in its edge over rivals.

**Where the table cannot test P1.** It contains no measure of any firm's "performance edge over rivals" at the moment of the release decision. The best available public measures are aggregates that are family-agnostic and time-varying: Epoch AI reports that open models lagged closed models by 5–22 months on benchmarks and about 15 months on training compute in its November 2024 report, that the lag was about 3 months in the ECI as of October 2025, and about 4 months (8 ECI points) as of May 2026. Those describe the open-versus-closed gap in general; they do not identify, for any one developer at any one release date, how large that developer's edge was. With eight families and a single observation date, there is also no within-family variation to exploit, and the outcome variable is not binary anyway — as the licence column shows, "open" ranges from Apache-2.0 through MAU-capped and revenue-capped community licences to research-only licences and a contractually anti-distillation licence, so a binary open/closed regressand would misclassify several releases. **P1 is therefore not testable with this table.**

**P2 — the propensity to open is non-monotonic (inverted-U, the "open source window") in the developer's share of LLM-compatible applications.** This is Habibi's theoretical prediction, quoted from the same abstract: "A key prediction from the theoretical analysis is an inverted-U-shaped relationship between the owner's size, measured by its share of LLM-compatible applications, and its propensity to open source the LLM." Habibi's abstract presents this as a prediction of the theoretical framework, not as an estimated result.

**Is the table consistent with P2?** Only in the loose sense that the pattern is not monotone in apparent size. Firms sitting in the middle of the distribution of ecosystem reach — Meta, Mistral, Alibaba, DeepSeek, xAI in its Grok-1 and Grok-2 era — have all released weights, while the two most concentrated proprietary platforms, OpenAI (for its frontier tier) and Anthropic (entirely), have not, and the largest-distribution releases are systematically the small ones (Muse Glimmer 30B, gpt-oss-20b and gpt-oss-120b) rather than the flagships. That shape is compatible with an inverted-U whose right-hand descending branch is populated by the biggest platforms, but it is equally compatible with a simple monotone decline, with a fixed-cost story, or with a security-externality story, and the table cannot separate them.

**Where the table cannot test P2 — and this is the decisive limitation.** There is **no reliable public measure of any firm's "share of LLM-compatible applications."** That quantity is an object constructed inside Habibi's own analysis for the models in that dataset; we could not locate any published, citable, firm-level time series of it, and we deliberately do not substitute a proxy such as app-store counts, API call volumes, or parameter counts, because none of them measures the same thing and all of them would be invented data. Testing P2 also requires **panel** variation — the prediction is about how a firm's own propensity to open changes as its share of compatible applications moves — and this table is a cross-section of eight families on a single date, so it has neither the regressor nor the variation. An additional obstacle is that the unit of observation is wrong for the question: the prediction is about a firm's decision, but several firms here (Alibaba, Meta, Mistral, xAI, Google) simultaneously run an open tier and a closed tier, so a firm-level "propensity to open" is not even well defined without an explicit aggregation rule. **P2 is not testable with this table.**

## B. Limits of this evidence

**Observational, not causal.** Nothing here identifies the effect of any prize, licence rule, or disclosure mandate. The table correlates a release decision with observable characteristics of a developer, and the release decision is jointly determined with strategy, safety policy, competitive position, and regulatory exposure. There is no counterfactual, no randomisation, and no instrument. In particular, the observation that firms disclose training compute inconsistently (DeepSeek for V3 but not V4; Meta for Llama 3 but not the Muse generation) is consistent with a prize indexed by compute changing disclosure behaviour, but it is equally consistent with disclosure being a marketing choice, a norm that decays, or a function of which competitor the firm is fighting at the time. The table cannot distinguish these.

**Family-level, not firm-level.** A "family" is a product line, not a decision unit. Alibaba serves `qwen3.8-max` through Alibaba Cloud Model Studio while also publishing the same model's weights under a bespoke licence that requires a separate licence above a revenue threshold. Meta runs a closed Muse Spark and an open Muse Glimmer. Mistral runs Apache-2.0 open models alongside an API-only "Premier" tier. xAI published weights twice and then stopped. Any binary firm-level openness classification — including Epoch AI's, which treats downloadable weights as open "whether or not the model's code or data are open" and explicitly flags the controversy over whether open weights suffice — necessarily aggregates release decisions with opposite signs. For the paper's argument this cuts both ways: it is exactly why an openness-indexed reward is a blunt instrument, but it also means the table cannot be converted into a clean firm-level treatment variable.

**Licence and openness status change over time, so every cell must be dated.** We observed at least six within-developer licence or openness reversals in the window covered: Gemma moved from a bespoke Gemma Terms of Use to Apache 2.0 with Gemma 4 (2 April 2026); Mistral's flagship moved from the Mistral AI Research License (research-only) through "Modified MIT" with a USD 20 million monthly-revenue condition to Apache 2.0 for Mistral Large 3; Meta moved from MAU-capped Llama community licences to Apache 2.0 for Muse Glimmer while closing its frontier model; Meta's open model also moved to a different Hugging Face organisation, which would silently break a naive data pipeline keyed on `meta-llama`; DeepSeek moved from a separate model-license agreement for V3 to MIT for R1 and the V4 series; Alibaba's flagship moved to a revenue-and-MAU-gated bespoke licence at the 2.4T scale; and xAI stopped releasing weights after Grok-2. Because of this, **a licence variable collected at one date and used at another will produce wrong answers**, and any "openness" indicator built on this table should record the observation date in the same row as the licence.

**Distillation claims are mostly unproven, and the burden of proof should be stated explicitly.** Only two cases in the table are first-party documented: DeepSeek's own R1-Distill-Qwen and R1-Distill-Llama models, which name their Qwen and Llama base checkpoints, and Meta's Muse Glimmer, whose named teacher is Meta's own Muse Spark. Everything else in that column is an allegation — Anthropic's September 2026 attributions against Alibaba, DeepSeek, Moonshot, MiniMax, Z.ai and Xiaomi, made "with high confidence" by an interested party; and the NSA/CISA/FBI advisory AA26-251A against DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun and Z.AI, which is an official government allegation that has been officially and publicly rejected (MOFCOM: "groundless and legally unsound"; MFA: "distorting facts"). **We therefore tag every such entry `contested` and never treat it as fact.** Two further cautions belong in the paper. First, absence of evidence is not evidence of absence: for most families the training data is not disclosed at all, so distillation can be neither confirmed nor excluded, and the two developers that disclosed the most about their training (DeepSeek, Meta) are also the two with documented distillation, which may reflect disclosure rather than behaviour. Second, the wording matters: "distillation" in these documents sometimes means extracting chain-of-thought transcripts, sometimes means training a smaller student model on a teacher's outputs, and sometimes means any use of a rival's outputs for supervised fine-tuning — Anthropic's own report acknowledges that "distillation itself is a legitimate training method." A reward indexed by verifiable training compute has to specify which of these it cares about, because the first is a terms-of-service violation while the second and third are standard practice.

**Open weights are not open source, and the table's licence column exists for that reason.** No developer in the table publishes its training data, and none publishes training code sufficient to reproduce a flagship; several do not publish training code at all. Meanwhile the licence column spans Apache 2.0, MIT, "Modified MIT" with a revenue condition, MAU-capped community licences (the Llama 700-million clause, the old Qwen 100-million clause), a bespoke revenue-and-MAU-gated Qwen3.8-Max licence, CC BY-NC 4.0, research-only terms (Mistral AI Research License), and an xAI licence that permits downloads while forbidding the outputs from being used to train another model. Two adjacent rows can therefore both read "weights downloadable: yes" while differing enormously in what a recipient may do, which is a direct challenge to any reward indexed on openness alone.

**Training-compute disclosure is sporadic and non-monotonic, which is a practical limit on the paper's proposed remedy.** The developers that have disclosed a usable figure are DeepSeek, for V3 only (2788K H800 GPU-hours; about USD 5.576M), and Meta, for Llama 3 405B only (3.8 x 10^25 FLOPs; up to 16K H100 GPUs). Google discloses training infrastructure at the chip-count level for Gemma 3 but no total figure, and parameter counts only for Gemma 4. OpenAI, Anthropic, Alibaba, Mistral and xAI publish no training-compute figure for their flagships in the sources opened. A prize indexed by verifiable training compute therefore has to solve a measurement problem before it solves an incentive problem: for most firms the quantity it would pay on is currently unobservable to the payer, and the two firms that did disclose reduced or stopped disclosing in their next generation.

**Selection and residual sourcing caveats.** The table covers only model families with substantial public documentation, in effect the notable-model population, so it inherits whatever selection that population has (withdrawn releases, renamed models, region-limited releases and internal-only models are all absent). Several negatives — "no evidence of distillation," "no weights found for the Qwen3.7 tier," "no training-compute figure" — are searches over the sources listed in each row, not exhaustive audits; we mark them as such rather than as claims of absence. Finally, the distillation dispute is moving quickly and involves government actors on both sides, so the `contested` tags in this table are tied to 2026-10-03 and to the documents cited, and would need re-checking before publication.
